01 — Background

Background

What is computational neuroscience?

Computational Neuroscience at its heart is the intersection between the fields of neuroscience and computer science, incorporating elements from mathematics and physics. Computational neuroscientists are most concerned with how to study the behavior and functions of the brain using computer models, specifically neural networks.

What is linguistic relativity?

Linguistic Relativity, also known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, is a theory that posits that a person's spoken language influences how they think about and conceive of the world. While the original strong version of the hypothesis is not very widely accepted anymore, some studies have shown a weaker form existing cognitively.

02 — More Terminology

More Terminology

Large Language Models (LLMs) +
Large-Language Models (LLMs) are artificial intelligence frameworks that interface with human language by generating and interpreting text.
Reasoning Models +
Reasoning Models are a variety of LLMs that are targeted towards solving logical and mathematical problems by generating a line of reasoning with steps that can be revisited and revised before its output.
Embedding Space +
Embedding Space is a multi-dimensional vector mapping where a class of items are plotted as vectors based on their semantic similarities, with closeness representing higher similarity.
Lossy Compression +
Lossy Compression is a method of data compression that reduces data size by eliminating redundant information, usually at the expense of quality.
Semantic Alignment +
Semantic Alignment is a procedure for accurately mapping conceptually-identical lexical items in one-to-one correspondences across a varied set of data, such as a set of different languages or physical representations.
Semantic Polarity +
Semantic Polarity refers to the positive or negative orientations that are assigned as vectors to lexical items and their corresponding concepts in an embedding space.
Semantic Resonance +
Semantic Resonance refers to the phenomenon where words, image and other communicative mediums trigger related information stored in long-term information to create deeper meaning.

This relatively newly explored intersection between linguistic relativity and computational neuroscience has led to many interesting research papers by scholars in recent years. This is a survey of five different studies with unique focuses in order to demonstrate how the above background information applies to new cutting-edge research across different social sciences.

Applying These Concepts to Scholarly Findings

03 — Gao et al. (2025)

Under the Shadow of Babel: How Language Shapes Reasoning in LLMs

So is the linguistic relativity claim true and different languages cause an LLM to think in a different way? Some researchers from Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence in Abu Dhabi examined this in their recent paper. This paper looks at English and Mandarin Chinese language data from a semantically aligned dataset BICAUSE in order to examine how each language is processed by an LLM. They constructed this dataset of 400 samples in each language through sequential causal chains, linkings of different related events that result in a final outcome (which can be introduced first or last). So an example of this in English that they give is:

Example causal chain (English)
"Aroma spreads, due to bread toasts, which originates from toaster heats."

The authors related 3 main findings from their research:

  • English has a typologically balanced distribution of lexical items. In Chinese, LLMs fixate more on sentence-initial connectives (such as 'and so' or 'once') along with causes as opposed to outcomes.
  • LLMs learned word order structures of causal sentences and overcorrected in applying them in infelicitous contexts.
  • When surface-form causal structures are learned, LLMs can begin to generalize deeper structure semantic properties and reasoning biases inherent cross-linguistically.

04 — Ray (2025)

Does Linguistic Relativity Hypothesis Apply on ChatGPT Responses? Yes, It Does.

Can this information in support of linguistic relativity be generalized more widely across more than 2 languages? Professor Partha Pratim Ray from the department of Computer Applications, Sikkim University in India takes this experiment to a larger scale, investigating how ChatGPT generates responses to stable prompts across 13 languages: English, Hindi, Gujarati, Marathi, Urdu, French, Spanish, Portuguese, German, Russian, Chinese, Japanese, and Arabic. To conduct this experiment, Ray created what he called "10 culturally salient prompts" that he fed into ChatGPT-4o for each of the 13 languages. 05 — Kauf (2024)

The Relationship Between Linguistic Representations in Biological and Artificial Neural Networks

So taking into consideration these findings from Gao et al., is it even valuable to computer model the complex language processing happening biologically? This dissertation by MIT doctorate student, Carina Kauf, gives an overview of the common threads and differences between how human and artificial systems process language. Kauf's thesis revolves around answering 3 questions for evaluating the effectiveness of LLMs in language processing using an mixed-discipline study of computational models, neural imaging and psychological behavioral experiments.

  • How are linguistic stimuli encoded into the language processing internal representations of an LLM?
  • How are linguistic stimuli encoded into the language processing centers of the brain?
  • How can these two representational systems be meaningfully related?

Having answered these questions, Kauf concludes that LLMs and humans both represent linguistic information well enough for the brain to be encoded very accurately with the assistance of computational modeling. Her thesis also provides evidence of semantic information being encoded mostly superficially, meaning humans process language through many interwoven systems of these layers, which LLMs can once again help model, especially when integrated with human cognitive research. This builds really well upon the findings of Gao et al. in establishing the existence of a stable semantic and logical framework that can possibly illuminate the smaller cognitive nuances cross-linguistically.

Gao et al. + Ray: language shapes thought

Both authors here argue that outputs of LLMs support variance in semantic alignment and reasoning biases cross-linguistically. Language therefore shapes thought, supporting the weak Sapir-Whorf hypothesis at minimum.

Dubey + Palmer: thought shapes language

Both authors here create frameworks that invert the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, instead relying on a layer of stable pre-linguistic meaning that is then transformed into an output language layer.

06 — Dubey (2025)

Language as Lossy Compression: A Multilingual Framework for Cognition and Artificial Intelligence

The scholarly work so far has mainly been in support of at least some weak form of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, however some very newly published work by independent researcher Akshat Dubey is trying to push back with a diametrically-opposed theory of language as lossy compression. As Dubey explains it, all LLMs often struggle with arithmetic while succeeding with generating code as well as creative and technical prose. This, he argues, is because language is inherently a compressed form of layer X, what Dubey calls "pre-linguistic cognition" which stores logical and mathematical reasoning. That more symbolic information is lost during compression, so therefore accessing these layers in X isn't communicable with natural language. Dubey refers to this as the inverse of the Sapir-Whorf claim: language doesn't shape thought, thought shapes language. This concept is illustrated nicely by figure 1 which uses a really thoughtful analogy, comparing layer X to a raw sound wave with different compression algorithms yielding different output patterns in layer L:

Dubey then uses his theory to make a set of 5 predictions about how AI systems would perform as a result. A prediction he hinted at from the beginning was that LLMs would excel at layer L tasks like code generation and writing style transfer, but struggle with layer X tasks like mathematical proofs, strategic gaming, and producing actually novel creative items. Additionally, examining reasoning task performances cross-linguistically, the differentiation in performance comes from "compression mismatches" rather than how hard the task is.

07 — Palmer (2025)

The Agnostic Meaning Substrate (AMS): A Theoretical Framework for Emergent Meaning in Large Language Models

Russ Palmer, another independent researcher, introduces his "agnostic meaning substrate (AMS)" a really similar conceptual framework to Dubey that seeks to prove the opposite of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. This is especially fascinating, as both authors reach very similar conclusions despite not citing each other's work. Palmer defines AMS as, "a latent, non-symbolic structure within large language models where conceptual meaning stabilizes independently of any specific human language, culture, or syntax." Differently from Dubey, Palmer does not use AMS to situate logical reasoning in an equivalent layer X, however there is still a deeper structure where. To explain the theory, Palmer lays out a very detailed series of 20 hypotheses to demonstrate stable semantic and analogical structures in different languages through a series of reasoning tests for LLMs. Hypothesis 5.8, for example, looks at semantic resonance continuity cross-linguistically in different semantic domains ranging from emotional statements to metaphors and idioms:

Conclusions

08 — Conclusions

Conclusions

This concludes a brief survey of the state of the linguistic relativity in computational neuroscience. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is still being supported and challenged in many interesting and novel ways, especially now that LLMs are integrated, providing a fertile testing ground for studying human language tendencies in a vacuum. The most important takeaway from this literature review is that this is still a very new field of research. Linguists, computer scientists, philosophers and sociologists working as Google data scientists, university professors and independent researchers, all have weighed in with their own experiments and tests involving LLMs.

Future directions of study

So where can future studies go from here? Well, the obvious next step is to keep testing LLM responses as more and more "improved" models come out from ChatGPT, Anthropic, Google, etc. With this could come a bigger surge of models that are trained on smaller languages outside of English, Mandarin, Spanish and other widely spoken languages. There are over 7,000 languages currently spoken, giving researchers a possible plethora of data to back up any arguments in favor of or against Sapir-Whorf.

Limiting factors at play

There is no established subfield exclusively studying language relativity, the mind, and computers, so it makes sense that the papers on this subject disagree on methodologies and conclusions. As with any new cutting-edge research, especially in the realm of neuroscience, it's also very difficult to confidently make claims without many years of repeated testing. Since LLMs are such a rapidly advancing technology, it may not be long until they start outputting fundamentally different responses semantically to some of these "universals" that researchers posed. So if that's the case, is it fair to still make a comparison with human language, where semantics drift over time but not as quickly?
